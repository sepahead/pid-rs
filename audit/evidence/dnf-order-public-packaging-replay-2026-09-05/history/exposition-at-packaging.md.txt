# From source collections to equality events

This note explains how the order used to label a partial information decomposition (PID)
corresponds to logical implication. It then identifies the assumptions needed to recover that
order from categorical equality events. The logic is classical. The repository formalization
connects it to an existing finite-event representation and records four counterexamples to
stronger statements.

axiom, and fresh-kernel judge in normal and optimized Python. The accepted source and a declared
comment-only variant each passed both modes. The [evidence record](../../evidence/dnf-order-formal-verification-2026-09-05/RESULTS.md)
preserves three earlier failures, the reporting amendment, and the later source-only tactic

## The mathematical objects

Let \(I\) be a set of source indices. In the examples, \(I=\{1,2\}\) or
\(I=\{1,2,3\}\). A **collection** \(a\) is a finite subset of \(I\). The notation
\(a\subseteq b\) means that every index in \(a\) also belongs to \(b\); equality is allowed.
A **family** \(\alpha\) is a finite set of collections. Thus \(\{1,2\}\) is one collection,
whereas \(\{\{1\},\{2\}\}\) is a family with two collections.

An **antichain** is a family in which no distinct collection contains another. Equivalently,
if \(a,b\in\alpha\) and \(a\subseteq b\), then \(a=b\). For example,
\(\{\{1\},\{2\}\}\) is an antichain, but \(\{\{1\},\{1,2\}\}\) is not.

The usual finite PID carrier contains the nonempty antichains of nonempty collections. Williams
and Beer specify this carrier and its order in Eqs. (4) and (5). These labels do not, by themselves,
select a numerical redundancy measure. Their \(I_{\min}\) and the later shared-exclusions
quantity are distinct choices. [Williams and Beer, arXiv:1004.2515v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.2515v1)

For the generic logical statements below, we also permit an empty family or an empty collection.
This larger domain makes boundary cases explicit. It must not be confused with the narrower
paper PID carrier.

A **Boolean pattern** is a function \(e:I\to\{0,1\}\). A collection \(a\) accepts a pattern
when every index in \(a\) has value 1. A family accepts when at least one of its collections
accepts. Write

\[
 F_\alpha(e)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \exists a\in\alpha\;\forall i\in a,\ e_i=1.
 \tag{1}
\]

Thus we use **AND within a collection and OR across the family**. This is a monotone disjunctive
normal form (DNF): changing a 0 to a 1 cannot turn an accepted pattern into a rejected one.
The empty family accepts no pattern. An empty collection accepts every pattern, so any family
that contains it accepts every pattern. These are the constant false and constant true cases.

Gutknecht, Wibral, and Makkeh give the same OR-of-ANDs map in Eq. (4.1), relate it to the PID
order in Theorem 1, and use characteristic truth assignments in the appendix proof. The present
generic theorem is a formalization of that classical correspondence, with explicit treatment of
empty families and collections. [Gutknecht, Wibral, and Makkeh, arXiv:2008.09535v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09535v2)

## Why the order reverses implication

Define the redundancy order by

\[
 \alpha\preceq\beta
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \forall b\in\beta\;\exists a\in\alpha,\ a\subseteq b.
 \tag{2}
\]

For each collection on the right, the family on the left must contain a collection with no more
requirements. Consequently,

\[
 \boxed{\alpha\preceq\beta
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \forall e,\ F_\beta(e)\Rightarrow F_\alpha(e).}
 \tag{3: G1}
\]

Here is the proof in both directions. Suppose first that \(\alpha\preceq\beta\), and that
\(F_\beta(e)\) holds. Choose a collection \(b\in\beta\) whose indices all have value 1.
By the order definition, some \(a\in\alpha\) satisfies \(a\subseteq b\). All indices of
\(a\) therefore have value 1, so \(F_\alpha(e)\) holds.

Conversely, suppose that every pattern accepted by \(\beta\) is accepted by \(\alpha\).
For an arbitrary \(b\in\beta\), form its characteristic pattern: put 1 exactly on the indices
in \(b\), and 0 everywhere else. The collection \(b\) accepts this pattern. Some
\(a\in\alpha\) must therefore accept it too. Every index of \(a\) lies in \(b\), so
\(a\subseteq b\). This supplies the witness required by (2).

This argument needs finite collections and families, but it does not need either family to be an
antichain or nonempty. The generic Lean statement does not require the whole index type to be
finite. The categorical statements later use finite index and value types.

For two sources, put
\(\alpha=\{\{1\},\{2\}\}\) and \(\beta=\{\{1,2\}\}\). Then
\(F_\alpha=e_1\lor e_2\), \(F_\beta=e_1\land e_2\), and
\(\alpha\preceq\beta\).

| Boolean pattern \(e_1e_2\) | \(F_\alpha\): at least one 1 | \(F_\beta\): both 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | false | false |
| 01 | true | false |
| 10 | true | false |
| 11 | true | true |

![The OR family accepts equality patterns 01, 10, and 11. The AND family accepts only 11. Thus alpha precedes beta, while the beta event is contained in the alpha event.](event-order.svg)

*Figure 1. The four cells list possible Boolean equality patterns; their area carries no
probability information. “Included” and hatching both identify accepted patterns. The order
correspondence is the one in Gutknecht, Wibral, and Makkeh, Eq. (4.1) and Theorem 1. Whether a
particular categorical domain realizes all four patterns is a separate question.*

## Why the antichain premise matters

For arbitrary families, mutual order need not mean equality. Antichains remove the redundant
collections that cause this problem. The second proposed statement is

\[
 \alpha,\beta\text{ antichains},\quad
 \alpha\preceq\beta,\quad\beta\preceq\alpha
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha=\beta.
 \tag{G2}
\]

To prove it, take \(a\in\alpha\). The two order assumptions provide
\(b\in\beta\) and \(c\in\alpha\) with \(c\subseteq b\subseteq a\).
Since \(\alpha\) is an antichain, \(c=a\). The inclusions then force \(b=a\), so
\(a\in\beta\). This proves \(\alpha\subseteq\beta\). The same argument with the roles
exchanged proves \(\beta\subseteq\alpha\).

Together, G1 and G2 give the third statement:

\[
 \alpha,\beta\text{ antichains},\quad
 \forall e,\ F_\alpha(e)\Longleftrightarrow F_\beta(e)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha=\beta.
 \tag{G3}
\]

In words, an antichain is determined by its answers on **all** Boolean patterns. Its collections
are exactly the minimal sets of indices that make its DNF true. The word “all” will matter when
we restrict attention to a categorical domain.

## Turning equality to an anchor into a pattern

Now assume that \(I\), every source alphabet \(\mathcal X_i\), and the target alphabet
\(\mathcal T\) are finite. The alphabets need not be the same. A complete categorical key is

\[
 x=((x_i)_{i\in I},u)\in
 \Omega:=\left(\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal X_i\right)\times\mathcal T.
 \tag{4}
\]

Fix an **anchor** \(z=((s_i)_{i\in I},t)\in\Omega\). It supplies the values to which other
keys are compared. Define the equality pattern

\[
 e_z(x)_i=\begin{cases}1&x_i=s_i,\\0&x_i\ne s_i.\end{cases}
 \tag{5}
\]

The target coordinate is not tested here. A 1 means “equal to this anchor's source value.” It
does not mean that the raw source value is 1, or that a sensor is correct.

The collection event and family event are

\[
 B_a(z):=\{x\in\Omega:\forall i\in a,\ x_i=s_i\},\qquad
 E_\alpha(z):=\bigcup_{a\in\alpha}B_a(z).
 \tag{6}
\]

These are sets before any probability law is assigned. They use the event construction in
Makkeh, Gutknecht, and Wibral, Eqs. (5)–(8): Eq. (5) uses a conjunction for a joint source
collection, and Eq. (6) uses a disjunction across collections. This is the categorical event
underlying their pointwise shared-exclusions measure. [Makkeh, Gutknecht, and Wibral,
arXiv:2002.03356v5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03356v5)

The first categorical statement simply unfolds the definitions:

\[
 x\in E_\alpha(z)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_\alpha(e_z(x)).
 \tag{C1}
\]

Apply G1 to this equality pattern to obtain

\[
 \alpha\preceq\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\beta(z)\subseteq E_\alpha(z).
 \tag{C2}
\]

This direction needs no support or pattern-realizability premise. Every key in the smaller
event satisfies a collection from \(\beta\); the order provides a collection from
\(\alpha\) with fewer requirements.

For a concrete example, let both source alphabets be \(\{0,1\}\), use source anchor 00, and
temporarily omit the target column. The raw source values and equality flags run in opposite
directions:

| Raw source values \(x_1x_2\) | Equality pattern \(e_z(x)\) | In \(E_\alpha(z)\) | In \(E_\beta(z)\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00 | 11 | yes | yes |
| 01 | 10 | yes | no |
| 10 | 01 | yes | no |
| 11 | 00 | no | no |

Every allowed target value has the same source-event membership in this table. No row counts,
independence assumption, or probability weights have been supplied.

## When event observations determine the order

Let \(D\subseteq\Omega\) be a specified domain. It might be the full product alphabet, a
known population support, or an empirical support. State which one is intended. We say that
\(D\) **realizes every Boolean pattern at \(z\)** if

\[
 \forall e:I\to\{0,1\}\;\exists x\in D,\ e_z(x)=e.
 \tag{7}
\]

Under this sufficient premise, the third categorical statement recovers both directions:

\[
 \alpha\preceq\beta
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \forall x\in D,\ x\in E_\beta(z)\Rightarrow x\in E_\alpha(z).
 \tag{C3}
\]

The forward direction is C2. For the converse, take any Boolean pattern. Premise (7) supplies
a key in \(D\) with precisely that pattern. Implication between the events at this key becomes
implication between the two DNFs by C1. Since this works for every pattern, G1 applies. We do
not claim that (7) is necessary for every particular pair of families; it is a sufficient
condition that works uniformly for all pairs.

There is a direct way to establish (7) for the **full** product domain. Supply, for each source,
an alternate value \(r_i\in\mathcal X_i\) with \(r_i\ne s_i\). For a requested pattern,
choose

\[
 x_i=\begin{cases}s_i&e_i=1,\\r_i&e_i=0,\end{cases}
 \qquad u=t.
 \tag{8: C4}
\]

This key has exactly the requested equality pattern. The proposed theorem takes the alternates
and their inequalities as explicit inputs. It does not infer them from a finite sample. Even if
each alphabet has two values, the constructed combinations need not lie in a restricted domain
\(D\).

Combining realizability with G3 gives the fifth categorical statement:

\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \alpha,\beta\text{ antichains},\quad D\text{ realizes every pattern at }z,\\
 \forall x\in D,\ x\in E_\alpha(z)\Longleftrightarrow x\in E_\beta(z)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha=\beta.
 \end{gathered}
 \tag{C5}
\]

Indeed, every Boolean pattern has a representative key in \(D\), so the DNFs agree on all
patterns. This conclusion can fail if either the antichain premise or the realizability premise
is removed.

## Restricting to the same target value

Define \(T_z:=\{x=((x_i),u)\in\Omega:u=t\}\). The target-restricted event is
\(E_\alpha(z)\cap T_z\). Intersecting both sides of C2 with this **same fixed event** gives

\[
 \alpha\preceq\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 E_\beta(z)\cap T_z\subseteq E_\alpha(z)\cap T_z.
 \tag{C6}
\]

If a key belongs to the left side, it belongs to \(E_\beta(z)\) and has target value \(t\).
C2 supplies membership in \(E_\alpha(z)\), while the target equality is unchanged. The
candidate proof reuses the existing `SxEventBridge` theorem that identifies its target-restricted
event with this intersection.

A key can match every source of the anchor and have a different target value. It then belongs
to every nonempty family's source event, but not to its target-restricted event. Source matching
does not force target matching. No probability or positivity assumption is used in C6.
A converse for target-restricted events is outside this packet: realization of source patterns
on \(D\) does not ensure realization inside \(D\cap T_z\).

## Four counterexamples that preserve the boundaries

The four boundary statements use three source indices. The third index is deliberately unused
by some families. It does not need to carry information for these logical examples.

**B1 — distinct antichains can have the same categorical event.** Give each source and the
target a one-element alphabet. There is one complete key, and every source always matches its
anchor. Put \(\lambda=\{\{1\}\}\) and \(\rho=\{\{2\}\}\). Both are antichains, they are
different, and neither precedes the other: neither singleton collection contains the other.
Nevertheless \(E_\lambda(z)=E_\rho(z)=\Omega\). Unconditional injectivity of the categorical
event map is therefore false.

**B2 — an alphabet need not realize every Boolean pattern.** In the same one-element setting,
the only equality pattern is 111. Pattern 000 is impossible. Thus even the full product domain
need not realize all patterns. This refutes automatic realizability, which is a different
stronger assertion from event injectivity in B1. There are no alternate source values to supply
to C4.

**B3 — binary alphabets do not ensure realizability on a constrained support.** Give all three
sources alphabet \(\{0,1\}\), keep a one-element target alphabet, and use source anchor 000.
Restrict the domain to equal source values:
\(D=\{000,111\}\). On \(D\), the events for \(\lambda\) and \(\rho\) coincide: both
contain raw row 000 and exclude raw row 111. The equality patterns realized by \(D\) are 111
and 000. Pattern 100 is missing. In the full product alphabet the two events differ: raw row
011 has equality pattern 100, so it belongs to \(E_\lambda(z)\) and not
\(E_\rho(z)\). This single statement records restricted-domain equality, full-domain
inequality, and failure of realizability. It assigns no probabilities to the two rows.

**B4 — the antichain premise cannot be dropped.** Compare
\(\gamma=\{\{1\},\{1,2\}\}\) with \(\lambda=\{\{1\}\}\). The first family is not
an antichain and differs from the second. Both \(\gamma\preceq\lambda\) and
\(\lambda\preceq\gamma\) hold. Their DNFs agree on every pattern because

\[
 e_1\lor(e_1\land e_2)=e_1.
 \tag{9}
\]

The larger collection adds no accepted pattern. Without the antichain condition, mutual order
and identical DNF semantics both fail to determine a unique family.

## Sets, probability laws, and finite samples

The thirteen statements concern finite sets, logic, and their correspondence. A probability law
adds another layer. For a declared finite law \(p\) on \(\Omega\), the mass of an event is
\(p(E)=\sum_{x\in E}p(x)\), and its positive-mass support is
\(\operatorname{supp}(p)=\{x:p(x)>0\}\). If two events agree on that support, their masses
agree. Their intersections with the same target event also agree on the support. Equal masses
alone do not imply equal events.

For a nonempty sample of \(N\) complete keys, the empirical law is
\(\widehat p(x)=n_x/N\), where \(n_x\) is the observed count. Its support contains the observed
keys. A population cell with positive probability can be absent from the sample. Event equality
on \(\operatorname{supp}(\widehat p)\) therefore does not establish event equality on
\(\operatorname{supp}(p)\), or population calibration of an estimator. None of the thirteen
statements assumes independent rows, proves convergence, or supplies a confidence interval.

For orientation only, with a finite law and a positive-probability anchor, the MGW pointwise
shared-exclusions quantity can be written in the repository's units as

\[
 i^{\mathrm{sx}}_\cap(t:\alpha)
 =\ln\frac{p(E_\alpha(z)\cap T_z)}{p(E_\alpha(z))\,p(T_z)}.
 \tag{10}
\]

Here the PID family is nonempty, and the supported anchor belongs to all three relevant events,
so the three masses are positive. MGW Eq. (8) uses base-2 logarithms; (10) changes the unit to
nats by using the natural logarithm. This formula and its numerical evaluation are outside the
thirteen targets. Averaging it requires the specified joint law or empirical law; obtaining PID
atoms additionally requires the chosen lattice inversion. [Makkeh, Gutknecht, and Wibral,
arXiv:2002.03356v5, Eqs. (8) and (13)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03356v5)

This note does not identify MGW with \(I_{\min}\), BROJA, continuous shared-exclusions
constructions, or KSG mutual-information estimates. The set correspondence does not prove
correctness of a Rust implementation, floating-point execution, an estimator, or a robot's
decision rule.

## A cautious category analogy

A temperature channel might report a fixed category from \(\{\text{normal},\text{high}\}\),
while an acoustic channel reports \(\{\text{quiet},\text{loud}\}\). Relative to the anchor
\((\text{high},\text{loud})\), the OR family accepts a record when either category matches;
the AND family requires both. The categories label inputs, not truth, reliability, or causal
importance. A target such as a separately defined machine state remains a separate coordinate.

Duplicating one categorical channel can produce a diagonal support like B3: two nominal
coordinates need not provide independently selectable match patterns. This is a synthetic
analogy, with no claim about a particular physical sensor law or field performance. Thresholds
learned from data define fitted categories and need their own fit/evaluation protocol. They
change the estimand; these logical statements give no guarantee that categorization preserves
the information in continuous measurements. For a practical sensor decision, a direct task-loss
or ablation comparison may be more useful than a PID.

A statistical application must also specify each row's observation window and target horizon.
A future target can depend on innovations outside the source window. The source-window length
alone therefore does not establish independence of complete source-and-target rows. The same-row
Boolean example here supplies no temporal sampling or concentration theorem.

## What this bridge can support next

At a fixed anchor and a specified domain, two families can be grouped when their restricted
events are equal. The counterexamples show why this can produce fewer event classes than
syntactic antichain labels. C1 gives the exact event map, C2 fixes the order direction, and C6
keeps the target intersection attached to the same anchor. These are useful inputs to a later
support-quotient construction.

They do not yet justify compressing a PID atom computation. That step still needs a precise
quotient carrier and order, the required lattice or adjunction properties, factorization of the
chosen cumulative quantity, and a theorem about the resulting Möbius inversion. Equal numbers
cannot be merged merely because they happen to coincide. A quotient can also change with the
anchor; averaging across anchors needs a common representation or a separate correspondence
theorem. Those obligations belong to a distinct proposed task. This note makes no novelty or
completion claim for them.

The practical contribution of the present packet is explicit scope: full-pattern logical
equivalence, its finite categorical realization, and the exact premises under which information
is lost when that realization is restricted.
